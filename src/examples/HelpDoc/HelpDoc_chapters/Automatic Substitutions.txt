[>c] # Automatic Substitutions

As you type, WareWoolf quietly replaces a few things with what you almost certainly meant:

- A straight double quote becomes a curly one, opening or closing to suit where it falls: “Get out,” she said.
- A straight single quote does the same, and an apostrophe inside a word curls the right way round, so “don’t” and “it’s” come out properly.
- Two hyphens in a row become an em dash: “wait” followed by two hyphens gives you “wait—”.
- Three periods in a row become a single ellipsis: “well…”.

If a substitution is not the one you wanted, press **Ctrl+Z** once. That puts back exactly what you typed and leaves the rest of the sentence alone. Press it again and you undo the typing itself, as usual.

You can switch any of this off in **File > Settings**, under **Automatic Substitutions**. The top checkbox turns the whole feature off; the ones below it turn off a single substitution while leaving the others working. Turning the whole thing off and on again brings back the choices you had made rather than starting over.

Two rules worth knowing about:

A quote typed immediately after a dash closes rather than opens, because interrupted dialogue is far commoner in fiction than a dash leading into a speech. And two hyphens turn into a dash the moment you type the second one, so a line of three or more hyphens used as a scene break will need an undo, or the Em Dash rule switched off.

None of this touches writing you have already done. It applies only to what you type from here on: existing chapters, and anything you import or paste in, are left exactly as they are.

To bring an older manuscript into line, use **Tools > Convert Straight Quotes Etc.** It runs the same substitutions over every chapter at once, reading each one as though you had typed it, so a converted chapter comes out the same as a retyped one. The popup lets you uncheck any substitution you would rather leave alone; by default it applies all of them, whatever you have switched off in Settings. Unlike a substitution made as you type, this one cannot be undone, so save first.

Searching is not affected either way: a straight quote typed into **Tools > Find/Replace** finds the curly ones as well, so looking for a line of dialogue still works whether or not the manuscript has been converted.
