[>c] # Find and Replace

Find/Replace sits under the “Tools” menu, or Ctrl+F (Cmd+F on Mac).
	Note that Replace All cannot be undone as one action, and across a whole project it cannot be undone chapter by chapter either. Save first.

# The Four Boxes
* Case Sensitive — off to begin with, so “cat” finds “Cat” as well. Tick it where the difference matters.
* Whole Word Only — “cat” stops matching inside “concatenate”. A term made of punctuation works here too, so “--” and “’tis” can be looked for as whole words.
* Use Regex — reads the Find box as a pattern rather than as text. See below.
* In All Chapters — searches, and replaces, right across the project instead of only the chapter you are in. Reference chapters are included.
A straight quote you type finds the curly one in your manuscript, so searching for “don't” finds “don’t” however it is written. A curly quote you type means that exact quote, which is how you hunt down one particular one.

# Use Regex
A regular expression is a pattern rather than a piece of text: it says what a match looks like instead of spelling it out. What a novelist mostly wants one for is finding something whose exact wording varies — every double space, every paragraph that ends in a comma, every “said Somebody” — or swapping two things around.
	These are the characters that mean something inside a pattern. Everything else stands for itself.
* ‘.’ — any one character.
* ‘\w’ a letter or a digit, ‘\d’ a digit, ‘\s’ a space or a tab.
* ‘\W’, ‘\D’ and ‘\S’ — the opposite of each: anything that is not one of those.
* ‘\b’ — a word boundary, the edge between a word and anything else.
* ‘\*’ — whatever comes before it, any number of times, including none.
* ‘+’ — whatever comes before it, at least once.
* ‘?’ — whatever comes before it, once or not at all.
* ‘[abc]’ — any one of a, b or c. ‘[a-z]’ — any lower-case letter. ‘[^abc]’ — any character except those.
* ‘(…)’ — a group: something to repeat as a unit, and something you can refer back to when replacing.
* ‘|’ — or, as in ‘cat|dog’.
* ‘^’ and ‘$’ — the start and the end of a line.
* A backslash in front of any of these means the character itself, so ‘\.’ is a full stop and ‘\\\*’ an asterisk.
So ‘  +’ finds every run of two or more spaces, ‘,$’ every paragraph ending in a comma, and ‘said \w+’ every “said” with a name after it.

# Referring Back To What Was Found
In the Replace box, ‘$1’ stands for whatever the first bracketed group matched, ‘$2’ for the second, and so on up to ‘$9’. ‘$&’ stands for the whole match, and ‘$$’ for a plain dollar sign.
	So to turn every “Smith, John” into “John Smith”, find ‘(\w+), (\w+)’ and replace with ‘$2 $1’.
	This only happens with Use Regex ticked. Without it a dollar sign is a dollar sign, and replacing with ‘$5’ puts $5 in your text.

# Things Worth Knowing
* Case Sensitive and Whole Word Only still apply to a pattern. Whole Word wraps the pattern entire, so ‘cat|dog’ with it ticked matches neither “dogged” nor “catty”.
* ‘^’ and ‘$’ mean the start and end of a line, not of the chapter.
* ‘.’ stops at the end of a line. To match across a paragraph break, ask for the break itself with ‘\n’.
* A pattern that can match nothing at all — ‘x\*’ where there is no x — is stepped over rather than replaced, so a Replace All cannot scatter your replacement between every letter in the book.
* While you are still typing a pattern it is usually not a valid one yet, and the line under the buttons will say so: “Invalid pattern: Unterminated group”. That is a note that there is more to type, not a complaint.
